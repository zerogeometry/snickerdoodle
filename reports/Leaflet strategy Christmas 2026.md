## 1. What to print

### Size and paper

| | A6 (105 × 148 mm, postcard) | A5 (148 × 210 mm, half A4) |
|---|---|---|
| Best for | handing out, counters, fair tables | letterboxes and book bags, where it has to explain itself with no one there |
| Why | fits a coat pocket and a hand; cheapest per copy; a parent can scan it standing up | room for the "what happens to their drawings" panel, which is the question a careful parent asks first |
| Our versions | `A6-christmas-FAIR`, `A6-christmas-GATE` | `A5-christmas-DOOR`, `A5-christmas-BAGS`, `A5-spring-BAGS` |

**Paper: 350–400 gsm, double-sided, no lamination.**

- **Silk** makes the product photos look their best and is usually the cheapest heavy card.
- **Uncoated (matt uncoated)** feels warmer and more "crafty", and you can write on it. It costs a little more and prints a
  touch softer.
- **Recycled or FSC** is an easy, cheap step that suits a brand about keeping things. Most printers below offer it.

My pick for Christmas is **350 gsm silk**: it's the cheapest card that's next-day almost everywhere, and the photos sell
the product. For the spring book-bag run, **300 gsm uncoated** is cheapest. Recycled costs about £140 more on 10,000; that's
your call.

## 4. What goes on a leaflet, and why

*(Rewritten on 17 Nov after you said version 1 looked bad. The full reasoning is in
`project-office/gtm/C14-leaflets/CREATIVE-BRIEF.md`.)*

**Version 1 failed for one reason above all: it never showed the magic.** It had photos of finished gifts, but nothing
showed that they came from a child's drawing. It looked like any personalised-gift leaflet. It also made the weakest
thing (a free £5.50 card) the loudest, and crammed seven messages onto each side.

**Version 2 is built on one picture: the drawing next to the thing it became.** A crayon robin on a scrap of paper, a
hand-drawn arrow ("made real!"), and the same robin on a ceramic ornament on the tree. You don't need to read anything to
get it.

**What the evidence says, and how we've used it:**

| Finding | Source | What we did |
|---|---|---|
| A picture draws the eye whatever its size; text gets read more the bigger it is. One clear picture beats a collage. | eye tracking of 1,363 print ads ([Wedel & Pieters 2004](https://cir.nii.ac.jp/crid/1362825894564575360)) | One before → after picture and a big headline. No collage on the front. |
| Five times as many people read the headline as the body copy; captions are read more than body text; before → after pictures fascinate. | Ogilvy ([summary](https://copyblogger.com/?p=33800), [imagery](https://www.shortform.com/blog/ad-imagery/)) | A five-word headline, "Their drawing, on your tree.", and a caption tag on the photo: "the robin they drew · ceramic ornament · £21". |
| The words beside a QR code matter enormously. "Scan to unlock your exclusive offer" got 7.3% scans; "Scan to join" got 0.6%, on identical mailings of 27,000 each. | Stannp/Equinox test (vendor) ([Stannp](https://go.stannp.com/blogs/understanding-direct-mail-engagement-rates-what-to-expect-how-to-improve)) | The QR now promises a specific result: "Scan to see their drawing on a bauble, in seconds", plus "no account". |
| QR codes should be about 4 cm with clear space, sit beside their promise, appear on both sides, and land on a page that works at once with no login. | Stannp; Royal Mail MarketReach ([Hillarys case](https://www.marketreach.co.uk/case-studies/hillarys-optimised-door-drop)) | QR on both sides: 39 mm (A6) and 51 mm (A5) on the back, 21 mm (A6) and 30 mm (A5) on the front, each with a 3 mm white border and its promise beside it. It opens the free preview, no account. |
| One strong offer or benefit, a clear "what to do and by when", and a deadline. | MarketReach ([5 steps](https://www.marketreach.co.uk/blog/5-simple-steps-successful-door-drop)) | One benefit (see it on your tree), one action (scan), and one tomato pill: "order by sat 12 dec". |
| Rivals lead with their category ("art fundraiser", "#1 solution") and rarely show the drawing beside the product. Grandparents are a common angle. | competitor scan (Artkive, Xmas4Schools, Cauliflower Cards, Art Projects for Schools, NOTHS sellers) | We lead with *your* child's drawing on the product, which nobody else does clearly, and say "send granny a ready-to-buy link". |

**Front (stop them, show them):**

- the before → after picture, with the ornament cropped in so the "after" is the biggest thing on the page;
- a five-word headline;
- the scan promise in tomato beside the QR: "Scan to see their drawing on an ornament. Free, in seconds.";
- the prices, named consistently: "Ornaments £21 · baubles £19 · free UK delivery";
- the deadline pill;
- the logo on a paper tag (on kraft, the "doodle" half of the logo disappears without it).

Before sign-off, an independent design critique checked the designs, and every one of its fixes went in:

- the free promise is now on the front;
- the "after" is bigger;
- ornament and bauble are named consistently;
- the small print is 6.3–6.6 pt, not 5.3;
- everything sits inside the 5 mm safe area (the build now checks this);
- the A5 back has fewer blocks.

**Back (make it easy to act):**

- **"Try it with their drawing, free."** and one line: snap it on your phone, no account, no posting the original;
- a big QR with its promise;
- **"Also made real"**: four small photos with prices (mug £18, cards £5.50, wrapping paper £23, 2027 calendar £22);
- the granny link;
- the free first card as a small coupon with the code;
- the last order dates, the privacy line and the small print.

The A5s add three numbered steps and the "what happens to their drawings" box, because nobody is there to explain.

**The spring book-bag version** leads with **"For mum and granny, drawn by them."** Book bags reach mums too, so the
headline gives them someone to buy for. It shows one family drawing with an arrow to the card, cushion and framed print
made from it. The back opens with **"For the drawings in this book bag."**, which
speaks to the parent unpacking the bag that evening. Its "also made real" photos use *different* drawings, to show any
drawing works.

**Every picture is a brand sample** from your brand-assets folder or the site. They're made-up drawings, with no children,
faces or real names. **Every price was checked** against the live site and the code on 17 Nov. The ornament in the hero is
the £21 ceramic ornament, and the £19 is the round bauble, so the leaflet says exactly that.

**What we left out on purpose:**

- **A "draw it here" box:** it tells a child what to do.
- **App screenshots:** unreadable at this size.
- **Any form or tear-off slip:** personal data on paper.
- **A bigger discount:** see the decisions.
- **Reviews or customer counts:** we don't have them yet, and inventing them would break the advertising rules. The real
  product photo and "free UK delivery, made in the UK" do that job for now.

## 5. Tracking which leaflet earns money

**How it works today:**

- Each leaflet has its own code: **MAGICFAIR, MAGICGATE, MAGICDOOR, MAGICBAGS**.
- Each code is a "free card" code, so a family typing it gets exactly what the welcome card gives, no more.
- `/admin/codes` shows each code's **paid orders and takings**. That's your scoreboard.
- Each QR link also carries a tag (`?from=fair`, `?from=gate` and so on). Today the site ignores it.

**The gaps (these need a small website change, decision 1):**

1. **A code only sticks when the card comes off.** A guest, a returning customer, or a basket with no card is told the code
   is for a first order with a card, and the sale isn't counted. So the scoreboard will undercount.
2. **Nobody has to type the code.** Signed-in first orders get the free card automatically. Many leaflet buyers won't
   bother typing MAGICGATE, and their sale won't show against it.
3. **QR scans aren't counted.** The site keeps no visit logs on purpose (they'd hold private gift-link addresses), and
   there's no analytics.

**The fix I'd suggest (about a day's work, with tests):**

- When someone arrives by a leaflet link, the site **remembers which leaflet for that visit** and **fills the code in at
  checkout** for them.
- A campaign code **always tags the order**, even when the card can't come off.
- The site **adds one to a counter per leaflet** ("fair: 37 visits") with no cookie, no address and nothing about the
  person.

Then /admin/codes would show scans, orders and takings per leaflet. Nothing about this touches a child's data.

**Until then:** each code counts the signed-in first orders with a card. Write down how many leaflets went where, and when.

## 6. The rules (plain English)

**Advertising to children (the CAP Code, which the ASA enforces).** A "child" is anyone under 16
([CAP Code section 5](https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/05.html)). The code says:

- don't urge children to buy, or to get a grown-up to buy for them (5.4.2);
- don't encourage pestering or undermine parents (5.4.1);
- don't aim "order now" ads at children (5.5).

**A book-bag leaflet counts as reaching children.** The ASA upheld a complaint about a pub chain's leaflet sent home in book
bags ([Greene King, 2017](https://asa.org.uk/rulings/greene-king-retailing-limited-a17-387925.html)). So book-bag leaflets
must be plainly for grown-ups. Ours say so at the top, and every line talks to the parent.

**"Free" and promotions** ([section 3](https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/03.html),
[section 8](https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/08.html)):

- If something is "free" but depends on buying something else, say so clearly.
- State the important conditions, any closing date, and the promoter's name and address.
- Prices must include VAT and say what delivery costs (ours is free).

Our small print covers all of that. If you ever add an end date to the free card, print it.

**Schools and safeguarding:**

- A school's grounds are private property, so you need the school's say-so to be there
  ([DfE](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/controlling-access-to-school-premises/controlling-access-to-school-premises)).
  Book bags need the head's (or business manager's) written OK, and some schools say no to all businesses.
- **Do:** drop bundles at the office; offer the PTA a code that earns it a share (the schools report); give the head a
  sample and the privacy promise.
- **Never:** speak to pupils, hand leaflets to children, run competitions children enter, or have forms come back through
  the school.
- **At the gate:** stay on the public pavement, hand leaflets only to grown-ups, don't block the gate, and leave if staff
  ask you to.

**Handing out in the street:** councils can mark areas where handing out leaflets needs permission, with a fine of about
£100 ([gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/council-powers-to-control-leafleting-causing-a-litter-problem)). That's usually city
centres like Broadmead, not school pavements. Check with Bristol City Council before doing a shopping street, and pick up
any dropped leaflets.

**Data protection (GDPR):**

- A leaflet through every door, or in every book bag, isn't personal-data marketing, so no consent is needed
  ([ICO](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/direct-marketing-and-privacy-and-electronic-communications/direct-marketing-guidance/identify-direct-marketing/)).
- A QR code to the website is fine.
- What would change that: a form on the leaflet, a mailing list sign-up that isn't a clear opt-in, or a drop chosen by
  personal data. None of that is on ours.
- People who've opted out of junk mail through Royal Mail or the DMA are only covered for those services. A local firm
  will still deliver to them.

**The Children's Code** is about the website, which already has high-privacy defaults and no profiling. Leaflets don't
change that.

## 8. Things you might not have thought of

1. **Each fair or venue can have its own code.** Venues that host your stand can earn on their code like a creator
   (`/admin/codes`, partner code). Print a short run with their code, or put a small round sticker over ours.
2. **Bring a sample table, not just leaflets.** People believe a bauble they can hold. Order 3–4 samples now (they're
   made to order and take about a week).
3. **The QR must be tested on paper**, at its real size, on an iPhone and an Android, before you order 10,000.
4. **Order a proof.** Most online printers show an on-screen proof for free. Check the cut line doesn't clip the QR or
   the small print.
5. **Leaflets don't keep.** The Christmas ones carry dates. Print what you'll hand out in three weeks, not a "cheaper per
   copy" 5,000.
6. **Recycling line:** "please pass this on or recycle it" is on every leaflet.
7. **Grandparents are buyers too.** At fairs, a lot of the people stopping will be grandparents. The leaflet works for
   them ("send granny a ready-to-buy link" works both ways).
8. **Say thank you in the parcel.** A small card in early orders ("tell a friend", with a code) costs pennies and reaches
   people who already love it. I can lay one out in the same style.
9. **Do it again after Christmas.** The calendar for 2027:
   - **Mother's Day** (Sun 7 Mar): book bags + fairs.
   - **Father's Day** (Sun 20 Jun): book bags again if Mother's Day works.
   - **Summer fairs** (Jun–Jul): PTA season.
   - **Back to school** (Sep): "the first drawing of the year".
   - **Christmas 2027**: book the Primary Times winter issue by early October, ahead of its early-November copy deadline.
10. **Keep it tidy and accountable.** Keep a one-line log of every drop: date, place, leaflet, how many. Two minutes now
    makes the spring decision easy.
